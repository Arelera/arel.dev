Try a paragraph before committing to a Chinese story. If you can follow what happens, with occasional help for a word, it may suit everyday reading. If you need a translation to connect nearly every sentence, choose something easier for that purpose. You can still study a short part of the harder story when it interests you.

A label such as “beginner” or “HSK 3” helps you find a starting point. What matters next is what happens when you read the actual text. Here are two original versions of the same scene to show what to check.

## Start with this short scene

Read the Chinese before checking the pronunciation or translation.

> [[zh:阿明去买面包。店里没有面包了。他买了两个包子，带回家和妹妹一起吃。|阿明去買麵包。店裡沒有麵包了。他買了兩個包子，帶回家和妹妹一起吃。]]

Can you say **what he wanted, what he bought, and who he ate with?** Those questions check whether the sentences have formed a scene in your mind.

*Ā Míng qù mǎi miàn bāo. Diàn lǐ méi yǒu miàn bāo le. Tā mǎi le liǎng ge bāo zi, dài huí jiā hé mèi mei yì qǐ chī.*

“A Ming went to buy bread. The shop had no bread left. He bought two steamed buns and took them home to eat with his younger sister.”

You might know everything except **[[zh:包子|包子]]** (*bāo zi*, a filled steamed bun). Looking up that food leaves the rest of the scene intact: he wanted one thing, bought something else, and shared it. A story written this way could be comfortable for you even with an unfamiliar item here and there.

But if **[[zh:买|買]]** (*mǎi*, buy), **[[zh:没有|沒有]]** (*méi yǒu*, not have), and **[[zh:带回家|帶回家]]** (*dài huí jiā*, take home) are all unclear, the same paragraph asks much more of you. You have to work out each event before the story can move forward. For regular reading, try a simpler passage or one using vocabulary you have already met.

## The same scene with more packed into each sentence

Now read this version. You already know the plot, so notice which phrases you understand from the Chinese and which you are filling in from memory.

> [[zh:阿明本想买面包当早餐，没想到刚到店里就听说已经卖完了。他只好改买两个包子，回家后分了一个给妹妹。|阿明本想買麵包當早餐，沒想到剛到店裡就聽說已經賣完了。他只好改買兩個包子，回家後分了一個給妹妹。]]

*Ā Míng běn xiǎng mǎi miàn bāo dāng zǎo cān, méi xiǎng dào gāng dào diàn lǐ jiù tīng shuō yǐ jīng mài wán le. Tā zhǐ hǎo gǎi mǎi liǎng ge bāo zi, huí jiā hòu fēn le yí ge gěi mèi mei.*

“A Ming had planned to buy bread for breakfast, but as soon as he got to the shop, he learned it had already sold out. He had to buy two steamed buns instead, and gave one to his younger sister when he got home.”

The extra difficulty is in the relationships between events:

- **[[zh:本想|本想]]** (*běn xiǎng*) introduces what he originally intended to do.
- **[[zh:没想到|沒想到]]** (*méi xiǎng dào*) introduces an unexpected turn.
- **[[zh:刚到……就……|剛到……就……]]** (*gāng dào… jiù…*) connects his arrival with what happened immediately afterward.
- **[[zh:已经卖完了|已經賣完了]]** (*yǐ jīng mài wán le*) says the bread had already sold out.
- **[[zh:只好改买|只好改買]]** (*zhǐ hǎo gǎi mǎi*) says he had to change what he bought.

You could recognize many characters here and still lose the sentence. For example, knowing **[[zh:卖|賣]]** (*mài*, sell) and **[[zh:完|完]]** (*wán*, finish) separately does not necessarily mean you understand **[[zh:卖完了|賣完了]]** as “sold out.”

If these expressions are mostly familiar, the second version might suit you well. If several need explaining, keep it as a short study passage and choose something closer to the first version for longer reading. Neither passage is an exam-level test; they illustrate how the same event can place different demands on a reader.

## Test another paragraph before choosing

Understanding a paragraph after reading its translation is useful, but it does not tell you how the next page will feel. Try a fresh paragraph from the story with the translation hidden.

1. Read it once, allowing yourself to reread a sentence.
2. Say what happened or what the paragraph explained. You can summarize in English; this is a reading check, not a speaking test.
3. Look up a word that blocks the meaning, then return to the sentence.
4. Continue into the next paragraph. Check whether that small amount of help lets you follow the story again.

**Keep reading** if the main events stay clear and lookups are occasional interruptions. **Study a short stretch** if a few useful words or patterns need attention and you want to understand them. **Try an easier story** if each sentence needs several explanations and you keep forgetting the previous event while decoding the next one.

There is no universal number of unknown characters that settles this. A name may be unfamiliar but easy to track; a short connecting phrase can change the meaning of a whole sentence. The [Extensive Reading Foundation's guide](https://erfoundation.org/guide/ERF_Guide.pdf) recommends easy material for sustained reading and distinguishes that from detailed study. The paragraph check above is a practical way to make that choice for yourself.

## Choose something you want to continue

Difficulty is only part of the decision. An easy story you do not care about can still be a poor choice. Try a familiar setting, a topic you enjoy, or a short story with a clear situation before moving to a longer book.

For a place to try the check, [Miaozi's story library](https://miaozi.co/en/read) has difficulty and topic filters, with audio and word lookup available alongside the text. Open a story and sample the beginning before deciding to read it all.

If the story fits but you are unsure which words deserve a lookup, our [guide to looking up words while reading Chinese](/en/blog/read-without-translating-every-word/) works through that decision in one passage. An easy story is a useful choice whenever your aim is to spend more time following Chinese without stopping at every line.
